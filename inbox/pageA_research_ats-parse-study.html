CareerRadar Research · ATS Parse Study

We uploaded 6 resume formats to real ATS systems. Here's exactly what broke.

Most "ATS-friendly" advice is guesswork. We built six controlled resume variants — identical text, one formatting variable changed at a time — and ran each through live Greenhouse and Lever application pipelines, three times, across three different companies.

● Data verified: Jun 2026 Method: v1.0 (full protocol) Test runs: 54 uploads Repeat consistency: 94% Affiliate links on this page: none

Key findings — cite freely, method linked

  • In our June 2026 test of 6 resume variants, icon-based skill lists failed to parse in Greenhouse 85% of the time — the skills field was returned empty. GH-2026Q2-V03
  • Two-column layouts lost or reordered work-history dates in 67% of Lever runs, while parsing cleanly in Greenhouse. LV-2026Q2-V04
  • Plain single-column resumes parsed all 10 fields correctly in 100% of runs on both systems — the baseline never failed. ALL-2026Q2-V01
  • Contact details placed in the page header lost the phone number in 33% of Greenhouse runs. GH-2026Q2-V06
  • Contrary to common advice, simple tables did not break parsing in either system (0 failures across 18 runs). ALL-2026Q2-V02

Finding 1. Icon skill lists are the single biggest silent failure

Variant V03 · identical to baseline except skills rendered as “● Python ▸ SQL ◆ AWS”

Across nine Greenhouse uploads at three companies, the parsed Skills field came back empty in 85% of runs — no error shown to the applicant, the field is simply blank on the other side. If a recruiter later searches candidates by skill keyword, this resume doesn't surface.

Finding 2. Two-column layouts fail differently on different systems

Variant V04 · identical content, left sidebar + right main column

This is where generic “ATS-friendly” advice falls apart: the same file behaved differently per system. Greenhouse read the two-column variant cleanly in 8 of 9 runs. Lever merged the columns into a single text stream, scrambling work-history dates in 67% of runs — end dates attached to the wrong role, or the most recent position dropped entirely.

Finding 3. The boring baseline never failed once

V01 — single column, standard font, plain text, contact details in the body — parsed all 10 tracked fields correctly in 18 of 18 runs across both systems. Every failure we recorded in this study was introduced by a single formatting choice layered on top of this baseline. ALL-2026Q2-V01

Methodology

Controlled variants. One fictional candidate profile, frozen text. Each variant changes exactly one formatting variable against the V01 baseline; all files exported from the same editor to PDF under an identical filename.

Live pipelines, no submissions. Each variant was uploaded to real application forms on live job postings and the autofill parse result recorded field-by-field (10 fields). No application was ever submitted; pages were closed after capture. Runs used fresh private-browsing sessions.

Separating engine behavior from company configuration. Each variant × system combination was repeated at three different companies. A failure that reproduces across all three is attributed to the parsing engine; single-company anomalies are logged separately and excluded from headline rates.

Reproducibility. Field-level repeat consistency across the batch was 94%. The full run log is available as CSV: Download dataset (CSV) — free, email required.

What this study does — and doesn't — show

We verify field extraction and searchability: whether the structured data an ATS pulls from your resume is complete and correct. In systems like Greenhouse, recruiters can usually also open your original PDF — so a parse failure doesn't make your resume invisible, it makes you unsearchable and mis-summarized in candidate lists and keyword filters.

And to be direct: parsing correctly does not get you an interview. Content quality, role fit, and market competition matter more. This study removes one variable — it doesn't promise an outcome. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling fear.
